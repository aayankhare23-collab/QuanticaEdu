Prealgebra · Lesson 4.7

Mixed Numbers

Solve this lesson, free →All lessons

Seven quarter-hops carry you across a creek from \(0\). After \(4\) hops your boot lands on \(1\), and three more put you past \(1\) but short of \(2\). You could call this spot seven quarters, \(\tfrac{7}{4}\), or just as fairly one whole plank and three quarters more, \(1\tfrac{3}{4}\). Same boot, same point, two names that are quietly equal. So how do you write a number that has slipped past a whole without landing on the next one, and how do you flip between the two names?

Problem
Your boot is at \(\tfrac{7}{4}\), past \(1\) but short of \(2\). How many whole planks have you completely crossed? Give that count.
Show a hint
  • A whole plank only counts as finished if your boot is fully past its far end. You sailed past the mark at \(1\), but you stopped before reaching the mark at \(2\), so the second plank is only partly crossed. How many planks did you finish all the way?
  • Count up the quarter-hops. Four quarter-hops carry you across one whole plank to the mark at \(1\). You took seven hops, which is four to reach \(1\) and three more past it. Those three extra hops have not yet reached \(2\), so only the planks behind the mark at \(1\) are truly finished.
Show the full solution
Each whole plank is split into four quarter-hops, so the marks fall at \(0\), \(\tfrac{4}{4}=1\), and \(\tfrac{8}{4}=2\). Your boot sits on the seventh hop, at \(\tfrac{7}{4}\). To find the finished planks, ask which whole-number mark your boot has fully cleared. The first four hops carry you exactly to the mark at \(1\), finishing one full plank. The next three hops, the fifth, sixth, and seventh, push you out onto the second plank but stop at \(\tfrac{7}{4}\), which is short of the mark at \(2\). So the second plank is only partly crossed and does not count yet. The largest whole-number mark your boot has passed is \(1\), so exactly one plank is finished. That whole \(1\) sitting behind your boot is the whole part of the mixed number. The three leftover quarter-hops are the fraction \(\tfrac{3}{4}\), and together they name the same point as \(\tfrac{7}{4}\), written as the mixed number \(1\tfrac{3}{4}\). The question asks only for the count of finished planks. \(\boxed{1}\)
Problem
Order slip: \(3\tfrac{2}{7}\) bands. Three whole bands = \(21\) beads, then add \(2\) loose. Write \(3\tfrac{2}{7}\) as an improper fraction in sevenths.
Show a hint
  • A whole band is not really one thing to the machine, it is 7 beads. So how many single beads hide inside the 3 whole bands before you even touch the loose ones?
  • Three whole bands are \(3\times 7=21\) beads. Add the 2 loose beads to get the total bead count, then write that total over 7, since each bead is one seventh of a band.
Show the full solution
Stop seeing the wholes as wholes and start counting the actual beads inside them. The machine measures everything in single beads, and a single bead is one seventh of a band, so I want one total bead count sitting over 7. Each whole band is 7 beads, and there are 3 of them, so the wholes carry \(3\times 7=21\) beads. On top of that sit the 2 loose beads. Altogether that is \(21+2=23\) beads. Every one of those beads is a seventh of a band, so the length is \(\dfrac{23}{7}\) of a band. As a quick sanity check, \(23\div 7\) is 3 with a remainder of 2, which lands right back on \(3\tfrac{2}{7}\), exactly where we started. $$3\tfrac{2}{7}=\frac{3\times 7+2}{7}=\frac{21+2}{7}=\boxed{\dfrac{23}{7}}$$
Problem
A lookout sits at \(\tfrac{47}{6}\) miles. Convert to a mixed number. Give the nearest post behind the hiker, the largest whole number below \(\tfrac{47}{6}\).
Show a hint
  • A mixed number is really a division. How many whole groups of \(6\) fit inside \(47\), and how much is left over? That whole count is the milepost she has already passed.
  • Divide \(47\) by \(6\). It goes in \(7\) times because \(6\times 7 = 42\), with \(5\) left over, so \(\frac{47}{6} = 7\tfrac{5}{6}\). The \(7\) is the whole part, so the lookout sits past post \(7\) but not yet at post \(8\).
Show the full solution
Start by asking how many full miles are buried inside \(\frac{47}{6}\). Each whole mile is \(\frac{6}{6}\), so the real question is how many \(6\)'s fit into \(47\). Since \(6\times 7 = 42\) and \(6\times 8 = 48\) would overshoot, exactly \(7\) whole miles fit, and \(47 - 42 = 5\) sixths are left over. That gives \(\frac{47}{6} = 7\tfrac{5}{6}\). The whole part \(7\) tells you she has fully passed post \(7\), and the leftover \(\frac{5}{6}\) of a mile means she has not yet reached post \(8\). So the lookout sits between posts \(7\) and \(8\), and the largest whole number still less than \(\frac{47}{6}\) is the post right behind her. $$\frac{47}{6} = 7\tfrac{5}{6}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{7}$$
Problem
A stretch is \(23\) beads, and every \(5\) beads make one full band (\(\tfrac{23}{5}\)). Divide \(23\) by \(5\): whole bands and leftover. Write the length as a mixed number.
Show a hint
  • The bottom number 5 is your group size, so you are really asking how many full fives fit inside 23. Count up in fives, 5, 10, 15, 20, and notice how far you can go before passing 23.
  • Divide 23 by 5. The quotient is the number of whole bands, and the remainder is the loose beads left over, which sit on top of 5 in the fraction part.
Show the full solution
The fraction \(\tfrac{23}{5}\) means 23 beads cut into groups of 5, so do that division. \(23 \div 5\) gives \(4\) with a remainder, because \(5 \times 4 = 20\) and that leaves \(23 - 20 = 3\) beads still hanging. So 23 beads make 4 complete bands with 3 loose beads dangling past the last one. Those 3 beads are 3 parts of a band that needs 5, so they read as \(\tfrac{3}{5}\) of a band. Stack the whole bands next to that leftover fraction and the length is \(4\tfrac{3}{5}\) bands. A quick check, four full bands is \(4 \times 5 = 20\) beads, plus the 3 loose beads makes \(23\), exactly the strand you started with. $$\frac{23}{5} = 4\tfrac{3}{5}$$ \(\boxed{4\tfrac{3}{5}}\)
Key idea
A mixed number like $1\frac{3}{4}$ just means $1+\frac{3}{4}$. You scoop up the whole pieces first and name only the leftover, so it marks the same point as the improper fraction $\frac{7}{4}$, only written differently. To turn an improper fraction into a mixed number, remember the bar means divide, so split $\frac{23}{5}$ by dividing $23$ by $5$, and the quotient is how many whole groups you finished (out front) while the remainder stays over the same bottom. To go back, smash each whole into bottom-many pieces, so $\text{whole}\times\text{bottom}+\text{top}$ counts every piece from $0$. No piece ever changes size, so both moves are the same bundling run forward or backward, and neither one moves the point.
Fun fact
In \(7\tfrac{5}{6}\) the space means "and" (\(+\)), a surveyors' shorthand. Once algebra arrives, juxtaposition means multiply (\(2x\)), so mixed-number notation is quietly retired.
Problem
Sensor at \(-7\tfrac{1}{3}\): the minus covers the whole quantity, so the sensor is a third below \(-7\). Give \(-7\tfrac{1}{3}\) as an improper fraction.
Show a hint
  • First locate where 7 1/3 lives on the positive side. It sits a third of the way past 7, between 7 and 8. Now reflect that whole spot across 0. Reflecting flips left and right, so whatever was just past 7 going up lands just past -7 going down.
  • The minus out front belongs to the entire amount, so -7 1/3 means -(7 1/3) = -(7 + 1/3) = -7 - 1/3. That is a third below -7, landing between -8 and -7. To get the improper fraction, write 7 1/3 = (7 times 3 + 1)/3 = 22/3, then attach the minus.
Show the full solution
The trap is treating the minus as if it only touches the \(7\) and leaves \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) positive. It does not. The minus sign belongs to the whole amount, so \(-7\tfrac{1}{3}\) is shorthand for \(-\left(7\tfrac{1}{3}\right) = -\left(7 + \tfrac{1}{3}\right) = -7 - \tfrac{1}{3}\). Notice that second sign flipped too. So the sensor sits a third below \(-7\), not above it. The attendant marked it a third toward \(-6\), which is the wrong direction. On the line, \(-7\tfrac{1}{3}\) lands between \(-8\) and \(-7\), a little past \(-7\) on the way down. A clean way to see it is by mirroring. The point \(7\tfrac{1}{3}\) sits a third past \(7\) heading up toward \(8\). Reflect that across \(0\) and up becomes down, so it must sit a third past \(-7\) heading down toward \(-8\). For the exact value, first build the improper fraction from the positive part. \(7\tfrac{1}{3} = \dfrac{7 \times 3 + 1}{3} = \dfrac{22}{3}\). The mirror image across \(0\) just attaches the minus, giving \(-\dfrac{22}{3}\). As a check, \(-\dfrac{22}{3} = -7.333\ldots\), which is indeed a third below \(-7\). $$-7\tfrac{1}{3} = -\left(7 + \tfrac{1}{3}\right) = -\frac{22}{3}$$ $$\boxed{-\dfrac{22}{3}}$$
Key idea
A minus sign in front of a mixed number covers the whole quantity, not just the whole part, so $-7\frac{1}{3}$ means $-7-\frac{1}{3}$, a third below $-7$, resting between $-8$ and $-7$. The safe move is to find where the positive $7\frac{1}{3}$ sits and mirror that whole distance across $0$, or simply hang the minus on the single improper fraction. That keeps $-7\frac{1}{3}$ from being confused with $-7+\frac{1}{3}$, a completely different number living between $-7$ and $-6$.
Problem
Potter uses \(2\tfrac{5}{6}+1\tfrac{5}{6}\) scoops. The fraction parts sum past \(1\), so carry a whole. Total scoops as a mixed number?
Show a hint
  • Keep the two columns apart for a moment. The whole scoops add to \(2+1=3\). Now add the sixths on their own, \(\tfrac{5}{6}+\tfrac{5}{6}\). That fraction sum is going to be bigger than \(1\), which is the whole point of this problem.
  • \(\tfrac{5}{6}+\tfrac{5}{6}=\tfrac{10}{6}\), and \(\tfrac{10}{6}\) is one whole scoop plus \(\tfrac{4}{6}\) left over, which simplifies to \(1\tfrac{2}{3}\). Hand that extra whole scoop up to the whole count. You started with \(3\) wholes, the carry makes it \(4\), and \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) of a scoop stays behind.
Show the full solution
Split each mixed number into its whole part and its fraction part and add the matching kinds together. The whole scoops give \(2+1=3\). The fraction parts give \(\tfrac{5}{6}+\tfrac{5}{6}=\tfrac{10}{6}\). So far the total looks like \(3\) wholes and \(\tfrac{10}{6}\), but \(\tfrac{10}{6}\) is more than a whole scoop, so it is not finished. Pull the whole scoop out of \(\tfrac{10}{6}\). Since \(\tfrac{6}{6}\) is exactly one scoop, \(\tfrac{10}{6}=\tfrac{6}{6}+\tfrac{4}{6}=1\tfrac{4}{6}\), and \(\tfrac{4}{6}\) simplifies to \(\tfrac{2}{3}\), so \(\tfrac{10}{6}=1\tfrac{2}{3}\). That hidden whole scoop has to leave the fraction column and join the whole count. The \(3\) wholes become \(3+1=4\) wholes, and \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) of a scoop stays behind. The total is \(4\tfrac{2}{3}\) scoops, which you can check as the improper fraction \(\tfrac{17}{6}+\tfrac{11}{6}=\tfrac{28}{6}=\tfrac{14}{3}=4\tfrac{2}{3}\). What you just did has a name, you carried, exactly like carrying a ten in column addition, except here a full group of \(\tfrac{6}{6}\) carries instead of a full group of ten. \(\boxed{4\tfrac{2}{3}\text{ scoops}}\)
Problem
Rail is \(5\tfrac{1}{4}\) spans. Cut \(2\tfrac{3}{4}\). Since \(\tfrac{1}{4}<\tfrac{3}{4}\), trade one whole for \(4\) quarters. Finish and give what remains as a mixed number.
Show a hint
  • The trade does not change how much rail there is, it just changes how it is written. One whole span is the same as \(\tfrac{4}{4}\), so trading a whole into quarters turns \(5\) wholes and \(\tfrac{1}{4}\) into \(4\) wholes and a bigger pile of quarters. How many quarters are in that pile now?
  • After the trade you are looking at \(4\tfrac{5}{4}\) minus \(2\tfrac{3}{4}\). Subtract the quarters first, \(\tfrac{5}{4}-\tfrac{3}{4}\), then subtract the wholes, \(4-2\). Put the two results together and simplify the fraction.
Show the full solution
The plan is sound, the only trouble is that the quarters column will not subtract as written, since \(\tfrac{1}{4}\) is less than \(\tfrac{3}{4}\). So we rewrite the rail without changing its length. One whole span is exactly \(\tfrac{4}{4}\), so take a single whole out of the \(5\) and turn it into quarters. That leaves \(4\) wholes, and the quarters become \(\tfrac{4}{4}+\tfrac{1}{4}=\tfrac{5}{4}\). In other words \(5\tfrac{1}{4}\) and \(4\tfrac{5}{4}\) are two names for the same amount of rail. Now the columns subtract cleanly. Quarters give \(\tfrac{5}{4}-\tfrac{3}{4}=\tfrac{2}{4}\), and wholes give \(4-2=2\), so what remains is \(2\tfrac{2}{4}\). Halving top and bottom of \(\tfrac{2}{4}\) gives \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), so the leftover rail is $$2\tfrac{2}{4}=\boxed{2\tfrac{1}{2}}$$ hand-spans.
Problem
Legs: \(1\tfrac{1}{2}+2\tfrac{1}{3}\) laps. Halves and thirds differ, so find lcd, rename fractions, then add. Check for carry. Total as a mixed number?
Show a hint
  • Split each amount into its whole part and its fraction part. The wholes are \(1\) and \(2\), so those give \(3\) laps right away. The fractions \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) cannot be added as they stand because the pieces are different sizes, so first find a denominator that both \(2\) and \(3\) divide into.
  • Use \(6\) as the shared bottom. Rename \(\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{3}{6}\) and \(\tfrac{1}{3}=\tfrac{2}{6}\), then add to get \(\tfrac{3}{6}+\tfrac{2}{6}=\tfrac{5}{6}\). Since \(\tfrac{5}{6}\) is less than one whole lap, there is nothing to carry, so just attach it to the \(3\) whole laps.
Show the full solution
Pull each mixed number apart into a whole part and a fraction part. The wholes are \(1\) and \(2\), and \(1+2=3\) laps. The fraction parts are \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(\tfrac{1}{3}\), and these are pieces of different sizes, so they cannot be combined until they share a bottom number. The smallest number that both \(2\) and \(3\) divide into is \(6\), so rename each one over sixths. A half is three of those sixths, \(\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{3}{6}\), and a third is two of them, \(\tfrac{1}{3}=\tfrac{2}{6}\). Now the pieces match, so \(\tfrac{3}{6}+\tfrac{2}{6}=\tfrac{5}{6}\). Check whether this spills past a whole lap. Since \(\tfrac{5}{6}\) is still under \(1\), there is no extra whole to carry over, so the \(3\) whole laps stay as \(3\). Putting the parts back together gives \(3\) whole laps and \(\tfrac{5}{6}\) of a lap, which is \(3\tfrac{5}{6}\) laps. As a single improper fraction that is \(\tfrac{23}{6}\), a handy way to confirm the work. \(\boxed{3\tfrac{5}{6}}\)
Key idea
Adding or subtracting mixed numbers is two small jobs side by side, wholes with wholes and fraction parts with fraction parts, once the fractions share a bottom. If the fraction parts pile up past one whole, that spare whole climbs into the whole count and only the leftover fraction stays, so $2\frac{5}{6}+3\frac{5}{6}$ becomes $5$ and $\frac{5}{3}$, which is $6\frac{2}{3}$. If you must take away more fraction than you own, break one whole back into pieces, turning a whole into $\frac{4}{4}$, and hand them down so the subtraction can finish. Carrying and borrowing are the same bundling trade, one read forward and the other back.
Problem
\(4\) planters each need \(2\tfrac{5}{8}\) bags. Convert to an improper fraction, multiply by \(4\), convert back. Total bags as a mixed number?
Show a hint
  • Rewrite \(2\tfrac{5}{8}\) as a single improper fraction before you touch the \(4\). Eighths times eighths stays in eighths, so the multiply step is clean.
  • \(2\tfrac{5}{8}=\tfrac{21}{8}\). Now \(4\times\tfrac{21}{8}=\tfrac{84}{8}\). Simplify that fraction, then split it into a whole number and a remaining fraction.
Show the full solution
First fold the mixed number into a single fraction. The whole part \(2\) is \(\tfrac{16}{8}\), and adding the \(\tfrac{5}{8}\) gives \(2\tfrac{5}{8}=\tfrac{21}{8}\). Now one box is just \(\tfrac{21}{8}\) bags, with no separate pieces to keep track of. Multiply by the \(4\) boxes, $$4\times\frac{21}{8}=\frac{84}{8}.$$ That fraction simplifies. Dividing top and bottom by \(4\) gives \(\tfrac{84}{8}=\tfrac{21}{2}\), and \(\tfrac{21}{2}\) is \(10\) with \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) left over, so the total is \(10\tfrac{1}{2}\) bags. This is why the separate-pieces road is dangerous. Multiplying gives \(4\times 2=8\) whole bags and \(4\times\tfrac{5}{8}=\tfrac{20}{8}=2\tfrac{1}{2}\) more bags. The fraction part did not stay a fraction, it ballooned into another \(2\tfrac{1}{2}\) bags, and writing the answer as "eight and two and a half" tangled together invites a slip. Converting to an improper fraction first never lets that surprise sneak up on you. The four boxes hold \(\boxed{10\tfrac{1}{2}}\) bags of soil.
Problem
Full day: \(3\tfrac{3}{4}\) km, and a half-staffed day gets \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) of that. Convert, multiply by \(\tfrac{2}{3}\), convert back. How many km, as a mixed number?
Show a hint
  • You cannot reliably take \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) of \(3\) and \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) of \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) separately and trust it. Turn \(3\tfrac{3}{4}\) into a single improper fraction first. Three wholes is \(\tfrac{12}{4}\), so \(3\tfrac{3}{4}=\tfrac{15}{4}\). Now you just owe yourself \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) of \(\tfrac{15}{4}\).
  • Multiply straight across, top times top and bottom times bottom: \(\tfrac{2}{3}\times\tfrac{15}{4}=\tfrac{2\times 15}{3\times 4}=\tfrac{30}{12}\). Now reduce \(\tfrac{30}{12}\) by its biggest common factor, then split that improper fraction into a whole number and a leftover fraction.
Show the full solution
First clear the mixed number out of the way, since the multiply rule works on a plain fraction. The three whole kilometres are \(\tfrac{12}{4}\), and adding the extra \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) gives \(3\tfrac{3}{4}=\tfrac{15}{4}\). Now take \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) of that by multiplying straight across, \(\tfrac{2}{3}\times\tfrac{15}{4}=\tfrac{2\times 15}{3\times 4}=\tfrac{30}{12}\). That fraction is honest but bulky, so reduce it. The greatest common factor of \(30\) and \(12\) is \(6\), and \(\tfrac{30}{12}=\tfrac{5}{2}\). Finally turn \(\tfrac{5}{2}\) back into a mixed number, since the question asks for kilometres in that shape. Two halves make one whole and there are five halves in all, so that is two wholes with one half left over, giving \(2\tfrac{1}{2}\). A quick sanity check fits the story too, half a staff should clear a bit more than half of \(3\tfrac{3}{4}\), and \(2\tfrac{1}{2}\) is comfortably in that range. The half-staffed day clears $$\boxed{2\tfrac{1}{2}}$$ kilometres.
Key idea
Multiplying is not like adding. The wholes and fractions do not stay in their own lanes, because every piece of one number has to meet every piece of the other. So you cannot multiply the wholes, multiply the fractions, and glue the answers back together. The reliable move is to drop the mixed form first, rewriting each number as a single improper fraction, then multiply straight across, or flip the second one and multiply to divide. Fold the result back into a mixed number at the end if the question asks for it that way.
Problem
Ribbon \(4\tfrac{1}{2}\) m, and bows are \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) m each. Convert, divide using flip-and-multiply. How many whole bows?
Show a hint
  • Before you can divide, the mixed number has to become a single fraction. Four and a half metres is the same as \(\tfrac{9}{2}\) metres, since two halves make each metre and there are nine halves in all. Now you are dividing \(\tfrac{9}{2}\) by \(\tfrac{3}{4}\).
  • Dividing by \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) means multiplying by its flip, \(\tfrac{4}{3}\). So compute \(\tfrac{9}{2}\times\tfrac{4}{3}\). Multiply the tops, multiply the bottoms, then simplify and read off the whole number of pieces.
Show the full solution
Start by turning the mixed number into a single fraction so the division rule can act on it. Four and a half metres is \(4\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{9}{2}\), because each whole metre is two halves and four and a half metres hold nine halves. The question \(4\tfrac{1}{2}\div\tfrac{3}{4}\) is really asking how many groups of \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) fit inside \(\tfrac{9}{2}\). Dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal, so flip \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) to get \(\tfrac{4}{3}\) and multiply. $$\frac{9}{2}\div\frac{3}{4}=\frac{9}{2}\times\frac{4}{3}=\frac{9\times 4}{2\times 3}=\frac{36}{6}=6.$$ The answer comes out to a clean whole number, which tells you something nice. The ribbon splits into exactly six \(\tfrac{3}{4}\)-metre pieces with nothing left over. You can check it by going the other way. Six pieces of \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) metre give \(6\times\tfrac{3}{4}=\tfrac{18}{4}=\tfrac{9}{2}=4\tfrac{1}{2}\) metres, which matches the ribbon you started with. So the count is the number of pieces, and here that count is whole and exact. \(\boxed{6}\)
Problem
A sign maker uses \(1\tfrac{5}{8}\) m of ribbon per letter for a \(4\)-letter word, starting from a \(7\) m roll. Convert, multiply, subtract. How many metres of ribbon are left on the roll?
Show a hint
  • Turn \(1\tfrac{5}{8}\) into eighths first, since every letter uses the same amount. Four equal letters means you can multiply that single fraction by \(4\) instead of adding it four times.
  • One letter is \(\tfrac{13}{8}\) metres, so four letters use \(4\times\tfrac{13}{8}=\tfrac{52}{8}\) metres. Simplify that, then write \(7\) as a fraction over the same denominator so you can subtract cleanly.
Show the full solution
Start by rewriting the amount one letter uses. A whole is \(\tfrac{8}{8}\), so \(1\tfrac{5}{8}=\tfrac{8}{8}+\tfrac{5}{8}=\tfrac{13}{8}\) metres per letter. Working in improper form keeps the multiplying tidy. Since all four letters are identical, you do not add four separate pieces. You multiply once. \(4\times\tfrac{13}{8}=\tfrac{4\times 13}{8}=\tfrac{52}{8}\). Now simplify, because \(52\) and \(8\) share a factor of \(4\). \(\tfrac{52}{8}=\tfrac{13}{2}=6\tfrac{1}{2}\) metres of ribbon used by the whole word. The last step is the subtraction, and this is where it pays to match denominators rather than guess. Write the full roll as \(7=\tfrac{14}{2}\), then \(\tfrac{14}{2}-\tfrac{13}{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\) metre. So half a metre is left on the roll. And that answers the maker's real question. Another letter needs \(1\tfrac{5}{8}\) metres, and \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) is far short of that, so no extra letter fits. $$7-4\times 1\tfrac{5}{8}=\tfrac{14}{2}-\tfrac{13}{2}=\boxed{\tfrac{1}{2}}\text{ metre}$$

Practice
An anchor leg is logged as \(\tfrac{29}{6}\) of a segment. Rewrite \(\tfrac{29}{6}\) as a mixed number in simplest form.
Show the solution
An improper fraction packs more than a whole into a single stack over the bar, so converting to a mixed number just means pulling out the whole groups. Each whole unit is \(\tfrac{6}{6}\), so I ask how many sixes fit into \(29\). Dividing, \(29 \div 6 = 4\) with a remainder of \(5\), because \(6 \times 4 = 24\) and \(29 - 24 = 5\). The quotient \(4\) is the whole-number part, and the remainder \(5\) stays over the original denominator as \(\tfrac{5}{6}\). The fraction \(\tfrac{5}{6}\) is already in simplest form since \(\gcd(5,6)=1\). Putting the pieces together gives four wholes and five sixths. As a quick check, \(4 + \tfrac{5}{6} = \tfrac{24}{6} + \tfrac{5}{6} = \tfrac{29}{6}\), which matches what we started with. \(\boxed{4\tfrac{5}{6}}\)
Practice
A staircase rises \(6\tfrac{2}{5}\) full turns. Rewrite \(6\tfrac{2}{5}\) as a single improper fraction in fifths.
Show the solution
A mixed number is just a whole part plus a fraction part, so I want to turn the whole part into fifths and combine. Each full turn is \(\tfrac{5}{5}\), and there are 6 of them, which gives \(6\times 5 = 30\) fifths. The fraction already carries another 2 fifths, so altogether I have \(30 + 2 = 32\) fifths. That is the shortcut you see written as \(6\times 5 + 2 = 32\) on top, with the denominator 5 left unchanged underneath. So \(6\tfrac{2}{5} = \dfrac{32}{5}\), and a quick check confirms it since \(32\div 5 = 6.4 = 6\tfrac{2}{5}\). \(\boxed{\tfrac{32}{5}}\)
Practice
A runner covers \(\tfrac{38}{7}\) laps. \(\tfrac{38}{7}\) falls between two consecutive whole numbers. What is the smaller of those two whole numbers?
Show the solution
Turn the improper fraction into a mixed number by seeing how many whole sevens fit inside \(38\). Since \(7\times 5=35\) and \(7\times 6=42\), five sevens fit with \(3\) left over, so \(\tfrac{38}{7}=5\tfrac{3}{7}\). That whole part of \(5\) tells you the value has passed \(5\) but has not reached \(6\), because the extra \(\tfrac{3}{7}\) is more than nothing yet less than a full \(1\). So \(\tfrac{38}{7}\) sits between the consecutive whole numbers \(5\) and \(6\). The runner has finished \(5\) complete laps and is partway through her sixth. The smaller of the two is \(\boxed{5}\).
Practice
A diver's tag reads \(-3\tfrac{2}{5}\) m: the minus covers the whole amount, so this is \(-3-\tfrac{2}{5}\). Rewrite \(-3\tfrac{2}{5}\) as a single improper fraction.
Show the solution
The tricky part is the sign. The reading \(-3\tfrac{2}{5}\) is not three and two fifths with a stray minus on the 3. It is the negative of the whole amount, \(-\left(3\tfrac{2}{5}\right)\), which is why we can read it as \(-3 - \tfrac{2}{5}\). So I convert the positive piece first, then carry the minus along. To turn \(3\tfrac{2}{5}\) into an improper fraction, I count how many fifths I have. Each whole is \(5\) fifths, so \(3\) wholes are \(15\) fifths, and the extra \(\tfrac{2}{5}\) brings it to \(17\) fifths. $$3\tfrac{2}{5} = \frac{3\times 5 + 2}{5} = \frac{17}{5}$$ Now I put the negative sign back on the whole thing, giving \(-\tfrac{17}{5}\). A quick sanity check: \(\tfrac{17}{5}\) is between \(3\) and \(4\), so \(-\tfrac{17}{5}\) sits between \(-4\) and \(-3\), exactly where a depth of about \(-3.4\) metres should land. \(\boxed{-\tfrac{17}{5}}\)
Practice
A flag is planted at \(\frac{23}{4}\) on a number line. What is the largest whole number to the left of the flag?
Show the solution
Turn the improper fraction into a mixed number by dividing. Since \(23 \div 4 = 5\) with a remainder of \(3\), we get \(\frac{23}{4}=5\tfrac{3}{4}\). So the flag lands three quarters of the way past \(5\), somewhere between \(5\) and \(6\). The whole numbers smaller than \(5\tfrac{3}{4}\) are \(5, 4, 3,\) and so on, and the largest of those is the one right below the flag. That whole number is \(\boxed{5}\).
Practice
A runner starts at a trail marker \(3\tfrac{4}{5}\) km from start, then jogs \(2\tfrac{3}{5}\) km more. How far from start is the runner? Give as a mixed number in simplest form.
Show the solution
Split each mixed number into its whole part and its fraction part, then add like with like. The wholes give \(3+2=5\). The fifths give \(\tfrac{4}{5}+\tfrac{3}{5}=\tfrac{7}{5}\). This is the moment to watch. Seven fifths is already bigger than one whole, so it does not belong sitting next to the fives as a leftover fraction. Trade it in. Since \(\tfrac{7}{5}=\tfrac{5}{5}+\tfrac{2}{5}=1\tfrac{2}{5}\), you carry one whole across. Now the wholes become \(5+1=6\) and the honest leftover is \(\tfrac{2}{5}\). Putting them back together, the runner is \(6\tfrac{2}{5}\) kilometers from the start. As a check, \(3\tfrac{4}{5}=\tfrac{19}{5}\) and \(2\tfrac{3}{5}=\tfrac{13}{5}\), and \(\tfrac{19}{5}+\tfrac{13}{5}=\tfrac{32}{5}=6\tfrac{2}{5}\). \(\boxed{6\tfrac{2}{5}}\)
Practice
Evaluate \(6\tfrac{1}{3}-2\tfrac{2}{3}\) and write the result as a mixed number. The thirds will not subtract cleanly, so you will need to borrow one whole from the \(6\). What is the value?
Show the solution
The thirds are the trouble. We want \(\tfrac{1}{3}-\tfrac{2}{3}\), but \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) is smaller than \(\tfrac{2}{3}\), so there is nothing clean to take away. Instead of stopping, we borrow. Take one whole out of the \(6\), which leaves \(5\), and turn that whole into \(\tfrac{3}{3}\). Stacking it onto the \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) gives \(\tfrac{1}{3}+\tfrac{3}{3}=\tfrac{4}{3}\). So \(6\tfrac{1}{3}\) is the same amount as \(5\tfrac{4}{3}\). Now everything subtracts in order. The wholes give \(5-2=3\), and the thirds give \(\tfrac{4}{3}-\tfrac{2}{3}=\tfrac{2}{3}\). Putting the pieces together leaves \(3\tfrac{2}{3}\). A quick sanity check with improper fractions agrees, since \(\tfrac{19}{3}-\tfrac{8}{3}=\tfrac{11}{3}=3\tfrac{2}{3}\). \(\boxed{3\tfrac{2}{3}}\)
Practice
Two legs: \(1\tfrac{1}{2}\) miles and \(2\tfrac{1}{4}\) miles. Line up fraction parts over the lcd before adding. What is the total distance as a mixed number?
Show the solution
First give both fraction parts the same bottom. Since \(\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{2}{4}\), the sum becomes \(1\tfrac{2}{4}+2\tfrac{1}{4}\). Now add the whole parts and the fraction parts on their own. The wholes give \(1+2=3\), and the fourths give \(\tfrac{2}{4}+\tfrac{1}{4}=\tfrac{3}{4}\). That fraction part is less than one whole, so there is no extra whole to carry over. Putting the pieces back together gives \(3\tfrac{3}{4}\) miles, which is \(\tfrac{15}{4}\) as an improper fraction. \(\boxed{3\tfrac{3}{4}}\)
Practice
Six planters each need \(1\tfrac{2}{3}\) bags of mulch. Convert to an improper fraction, then multiply by \(6\). How many bags total?
Show the solution
Start by turning the mixed number into one fraction so the multiplication is clean. One whole is \(\tfrac{3}{3}\), so \(1\tfrac{2}{3}=\tfrac{3}{3}+\tfrac{2}{3}=\tfrac{5}{3}\). Now multiply by the six planters. Treat \(6\) as \(\tfrac{6}{1}\) and multiply straight across, \(6\times\tfrac{5}{3}=\tfrac{6\times 5}{1\times 3}=\tfrac{30}{3}\). Finally simplify, and since \(30\div 3=10\) the fraction collapses to a whole number. The improper fraction was the shortcut here, it let the six and the three cancel into a tidy count instead of adding \(1\tfrac{2}{3}\) six separate times. The row of planters needs \(\boxed{10}\) bags of mulch.
Practice
One press pass uses \(2\tfrac{2}{3}\) oz of ink, and a proof run needs \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) of a pass. Convert, then multiply. How many ounces does the proof run use?
Show the solution
Start by turning the mixed number into a single fraction. The whole number 2 is \(\tfrac{6}{3}\), and adding the \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) gives \(2\tfrac{2}{3}=\tfrac{8}{3}\). Now the problem is a clean product of two fractions, \(\tfrac{3}{4}\times\tfrac{8}{3}\). Multiplying across the tops and across the bottoms gives \(\tfrac{3\times 8}{4\times 3}=\tfrac{24}{12}\). That fraction is really a whole number, since \(24\div 12=2\). So the proof run uses \(\boxed{2}\) ounces of ink.
Practice
A ribbon is \(3\tfrac{3}{4}\) m. Cut into \(1\tfrac{1}{2}\) m pieces. Convert both to improper fractions, divide, write the result as a mixed number. How many pieces fit?
Show the solution
First change both mixed numbers into improper fractions. For \(3\tfrac{3}{4}\), multiply the whole part by the denominator and add the top, giving \(3\times 4+3=15\), so it is \(\tfrac{15}{4}\). For \(1\tfrac{1}{2}\), the same move gives \(1\times 2+1=3\), so it is \(\tfrac{3}{2}\). Now the problem is \(\tfrac{15}{4}\div\tfrac{3}{2}\). Dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal, so flip the second fraction and multiply. $$\frac{15}{4}\div\frac{3}{2}=\frac{15}{4}\times\frac{2}{3}=\frac{30}{12}=\frac{5}{2}$$ Finally, write \(\tfrac{5}{2}\) as a mixed number. Two goes into five twice with one left over, so it is \(2\tfrac{1}{2}\). That matches the picture too, since two full pieces use \(3\) meters and the leftover \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) meter is exactly half of a \(1\tfrac{1}{2}\) meter piece. \(\boxed{2\tfrac{1}{2}}\)
Practice
One loop lap is \(4\tfrac{2}{3}\) km, and two runners split it equally. How long is each leg? Give as a mixed number in km.
Show the solution
Start by turning the mixed number into an improper fraction. Three thirds make one whole, so four wholes hold twelve thirds, and adding the extra two thirds gives \(4\tfrac{2}{3}=\tfrac{14}{3}\).

Now split that lap into two equal legs by dividing by 2. Dividing by 2 is the same as multiplying by \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), so \(\tfrac{14}{3}\div 2=\tfrac{14}{3}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{14}{6}\).

Both 14 and 6 share a factor of 2, so \(\tfrac{14}{6}=\tfrac{7}{3}\). Since three goes into seven twice with one left over, each leg is \(2\tfrac{1}{3}\) kilometers long. As a quick check, two legs of \(2\tfrac{1}{3}\) add to \(4\tfrac{2}{3}\), the whole lap, so the split is right.

\(\boxed{2\tfrac{1}{3}\text{ km}}\)
Practice
A full batch needs \(2\tfrac{1}{4}\) cups of flour. You make \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) of a batch. How many cups do you need? Give as a fraction in simplest form.
Show the solution
Making \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) of a batch means taking \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) of the flour, and the word "of" here is a signal to multiply. So you need \(\tfrac{1}{3}\times 2\tfrac{1}{4}\). Mixed numbers are awkward to multiply directly, so convert first. The whole number \(2\) is the same as \(\tfrac{8}{4}\), and adding the \(\tfrac{1}{4}\) gives \(2\tfrac{1}{4}=\tfrac{9}{4}\). Now multiply straight across, top times top and bottom times bottom. $$\frac{1}{3}\times\frac{9}{4}=\frac{1\times 9}{3\times 4}=\frac{9}{12}.$$ Both \(9\) and \(12\) share a factor of \(3\), so \(\tfrac{9}{12}=\tfrac{3}{4}\). You need three quarters of a cup of flour, which makes sense since a third of a batch should use a good deal less than the full \(2\tfrac{1}{4}\) cups. \(\boxed{\tfrac{3}{4}}\)
Practice
A board is \(5\tfrac{1}{2}\) ft. Two side brackets, each \(1\tfrac{3}{4}\) ft, are cut off. How many feet of board remain?
Show the solution
Start with the two pieces the builder removes. Each is \(1\tfrac{3}{4}=\tfrac{7}{4}\) feet, and there are two of them, so together they use \(2\times\tfrac{7}{4}=\tfrac{14}{4}=\tfrac{7}{2}=3\tfrac{1}{2}\) feet. Now subtract that from the original board. The board is \(5\tfrac{1}{2}\) feet, so what remains is \(5\tfrac{1}{2}-3\tfrac{1}{2}\). The two halves match and cancel, leaving \(5-3=2\) feet. So \(2\) feet of board are left over. \(\boxed{2}\)
