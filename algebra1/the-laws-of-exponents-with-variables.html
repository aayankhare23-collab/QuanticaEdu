Algebra I · Lesson 3.1

The Laws of Exponents with Variables

Solve this lesson, free →All lessons

1.5 built the exponent laws out of counting, and every one of those counts still works when the base is a letter. A letter stands for a number you have not named yet, so \(k^5\) is five factors of \(k\) and nothing in the argument changes. The new work is finding the two places the laws stop.

Problem
\(m^3\cdot m\cdot m^4\) is a row of factors of \(m\) with nothing but multiplication in it. Written as a single power it is \(m^k\). Count the factors and enter \(k\).
Show a hint
  • Write out the factors of \(m\) in each piece, then count the whole row.
  • A letter with no exponent written is one factor.
Show the full solution
The bare \(m\) looks like it carries nothing, and skipping it gives 7. It is one factor, since \(m=m^1\) from 1.5, so the row is 3 factors then 1 then 4, and joining rows adds the counts to give \(\boxed{8}\).
Key idea
The product rule. The product rule from 1.5 carries over untouched, \(x^m\cdot x^n=x^{m+n}\), because the count never asked what the base was. Two wrong turns to name. \(a^3\cdot a^4\) is \(a^7\) and not \(a^{12}\), because exponents count factors and joining rows adds counts instead of multiplying them. And it is not \(2a^7\), because nothing was added, the row just got longer.
Problem
A student writes \(n^2+n^5=n^7\), adding the exponents. Test the claim by picking a number, the way 1.4 did. Evaluate \(n^2+n^5\) at \(n=2\) and enter the value.
Show a hint
  • \(n^2\) and \(n^5\) are separate terms, so work out each one and then add.
  • For comparison, \(n^7\) at \(n=2\) is 128.
Show the full solution
Adding the exponents looks like the product rule, but the product rule applies to a product and this is a sum. At \(n=2\) the claim predicts \(n^7=128\), while the actual value is \(4+32\). One mismatch is enough to kill the claim, and the value you enter is \(\boxed{36}\).
Key idea
Two conditions. The laws need a shared base, and they need multiplication. \(a^3b^4\) counts two different things, so there is no single power to collapse it into. \(a^3+a^4\) is not \(a^7\) either. A plus sign joins terms, not rows of factors, and \(a^3\) and \(a^4\) are not even like terms in the 2.4 sense, so they stay apart. Both expressions are already as short as they get.
Problem
Multiply \((4c^5)(7c^3)\) into a single term. The numbers out front and the powers of \(c\) do not get the same treatment, so decide what happens to each before you write anything. Enter the result, typed like \(6y^2\).
Show a hint
  • Gather the numbers together and the \(c\) factors together, the 1.3 way.
  • The 4 and the 7 are factors. The 5 and the 3 are counts.
Show the full solution
Treating both halves alike gives \(28c^{15}\) or \(11c^8\), and each one applies the wrong operation to half the term. Coefficients are factors so they multiply, exponents are counts so they add, giving \(\boxed{28c^8}\).
Key idea
Monomial products. A coefficient is one more factor, so the reordering from 1.3 lets you gather numbers with numbers and letters with letters. In \((3a^4)(5a^2)\) the 3 and the 5 are factors and get multiplied, while the 4 and the 2 are counts and get added, so the term is \(15a^6\). The two halves of that line look different because they are different operations.
Problem
In \(\frac{z^{11}}{z^{4}}\), every factor on the bottom cancels a matching factor on top. What survives is a single power \(z^k\). Enter \(k\).
Show a hint
  • Four factors cancel four factors. How many of the eleven are left?
Show the full solution
The slip here is reporting the count that got cancelled and answering 4. The four factors on the bottom cancel four of the eleven factors on top, so the number of factors left over is \(11-4=\boxed{7}\).
Problem
Simplify \(\frac{54k^{10}}{6k^{3}}\) to a single term. The number part and the \(k\) part are handled separately. Enter the coefficient of the result.
Show a hint
  • Split it into a number part and a \(k\) part.
  • The coefficients divide. Only the exponents subtract.
Show the full solution
The trap is subtracting the coefficients to get 48. Subtraction belongs to the exponents, not to the numbers out front. The coefficients divide, \(54\div 6=9\), and the exponents subtract, \(10-3=7\), so the term is \(9k^{7}\) and the coefficient is \(\boxed{9}\).
Key idea
The quotient rule. The quotient rule from 1.5 survives the switch too, \(\frac{x^m}{x^n}=x^{m-n}\). Each factor on the bottom cancels a matching factor on top, and what is left is the leftover count. With coefficients the two halves split again, the numbers divide and the counts subtract, so \(\frac{20a^7}{4a^3}=5a^4\). Keep the larger exponent on top for now. 3.2 takes that guardrail off.
Problem
\((r^4)^6\) means six copies of \(r^4\) multiplied together. Written as one power it is \(r^k\). Count the factors of \(r\) in that whole product and enter \(k\).
Show a hint
  • Each copy of \(r^4\) contributes four factors of \(r\).
Show the full solution
Adding the exponents gives 10, which is the product rule used where it does not apply. The 6 counts copies of a four-factor block rather than extra factors, so the total is \(4\cdot 6=\boxed{24}\).
Key idea
Power of a power. \((x^m)^n=x^{mn}\). The outer exponent counts copies of a block, not extra factors. That one word, copies, is the whole difference from the product rule. Multiplying two powers sets rows side by side and the counts add. Raising a power stacks whole copies and the counts multiply. So \(a^4\cdot a^3\) is \(a^7\) while \((a^4)^3\) is \(a^{12}\).
Problem
Both \(q^5\cdot q^4\) and \((q^5)^4\) collapse to a single power of \(q\), and they do not collapse the same way. Write each one as a single power of \(q\). Take the exponent on \(q\) in the collapsed form of \((q^5)^4\) and subtract the exponent on \(q\) in the collapsed form of \(q^5\cdot q^4\). Enter that one number.
Show a hint
  • One expression sets two rows side by side. The other makes four copies of one row.
  • \(5+4\) and \(5\cdot 4\) are not the same number.
Show the full solution
Reading the two as the same thing gives a difference of 0, which is exactly the confusion. Side by side gives \(q^9\), four copies gives \(q^{20}\), and \(20-9=\boxed{11}\).
Problem
Expand \((3b^7)^3\) into a single term. The outer exponent makes three copies of everything inside the parentheses, the 3 in front included. Enter the coefficient of the result.
Show a hint
  • Write the three copies out. What sits inside each one?
Show the full solution
The common miss is leaving the coefficient alone and writing \(3b^{21}\). Three copies of \(3b^7\) hold three 3s and twenty-one \(b\)s, so the term is \(27b^{21}\) and the coefficient is \(\boxed{27}\).
Key idea
Power of a product. \((ca^m)^n=c^n a^{mn}\). The outer exponent makes \(n\) copies of the whole term, and the coefficient sits inside every copy, so it gets raised too. \((2a^3)^4\) is four copies of \(2a^3\), which holds four 2s and twelve \(a\)s, so it is \(16a^{12}\). Writing \(2a^{12}\) keeps only one of those four 2s. This is the \((ab)^n=a^nb^n\) cousin from 1.5 with a number playing one of the parts.
Problem
Expand \((u^3w^8)^4\). Each base carries its own exponent through the expansion. Enter the exponent on \(w\) in the result.
Show a hint
  • Four copies of \(u^3w^8\) reorder into all the \(u\) factors and then all the \(w\) factors.
Show the full solution
Applying the outer 4 to the first base only leaves \(w^8\) untouched, and that is the usual slip. Every copy contains eight \(w\) factors, so four copies give \(u^{12}w^{32}\) and the exponent on \(w\) is \(\boxed{32}\).
Key idea
Each base carries its own exponent. \((x^m y^n)^k=x^{mk}y^{nk}\), because the copies split by 1.3 into all the \(x\) factors and then all the \(y\) factors, and nothing crosses between them. For a mixed expression that fixes the order of work. Clear every parenthesis first with the power rules, then multiply across the top, then cancel. In that order each law runs on its own line and you never carry an ugly middle step. Side by side adds, copies multiply, cancelling subtracts, and a plus sign means none of this applies.
Problem
Simplify \((2y^4)^3\cdot 5y^2\) to a single term. Clear the parentheses first, then multiply. Enter the result, typed like \(6k^5\).
Show a hint
  • The cube reaches the 2 as well as the \(y^4\).
  • Once the parentheses are gone it is one product of two monomials.
Show the full solution
Skipping the \(2^3\) gives \(2y^{12}\cdot 5y^2=10y^{14}\), which is the whole trap. The cube makes three copies of \(2y^4\), so the parentheses clear to \(8y^{12}\), and then \(8\cdot 5=40\) with \(12+2=14\), giving \(\boxed{40y^{14}}\).
Problem
Simplify \(\frac{(5f^4)^2\cdot f^3}{5f^{6}}\) to a single term, then evaluate that term at \(f=2\). Enter the value, a single number.
Show a hint
  • Clear the parentheses first, then build one term on top before you divide.
  • Check what the outside exponent does to the 5, not just to \(f^4\).
Show the full solution
The trap is treating the outside exponent as if it only reaches \(f^4\). It reaches the 5 as well, so \((5f^4)^2=25f^8\), not \(5f^8\). The top becomes \(25f^8\cdot f^3=25f^{11}\), and dividing by \(5f^6\) gives \(5f^5\). At \(f=2\) that is \(5\cdot 32=\boxed{160}\).
Fun fact
The word exponent is older than the symbol. Michael Stifel coined it in 1544, lining the powers of 2 up beside their exponents and noticing that multiplying in one column matched adding in the other. Logarithms grew from that.

Every exponent in this lesson was a positive whole number, held there on purpose by keeping the larger exponent on top of every quotient. 3.2 removes that guardrail. Once the bottom is allowed to win, \(x^0\) and \(x^{-3}\) have to mean something, and the meaning comes from refusing to let these laws break.

Practice these ideas

Practice
Write \(g^7\cdot g^8\) as a single power \(g^k\) and enter \(k\).
Show the solution
Multiplying the exponents gives \(g^{56}\), which counts copies that are not there. Joining two rows adds the counts, \(7+8=\boxed{15}\).
Practice
A student claims \(d^2+d^4=d^6\). Test it the 1.4 way. Evaluate \(d^2+d^4\) at \(d=3\) and enter the value.
Show the solution
A plus sign joins terms rather than rows of factors, so nothing combines. At \(d=3\) the claim says \(3^6=729\) while \(9+81\) says \(\boxed{90}\).
Practice
A student claims \(a^4b^2=(ab)^6\). Test the claim by evaluating \(a^4b^2\) at \(a=2\) and \(b=3\), and enter the value.
Show the solution
The product rule adds exponents only when the powers share a base, and \(a\) and \(b\) are different bases, so \(a^4b^2\) does not collapse into \((ab)^6\). The claim would give \((ab)^6=6^6=46656\), but evaluating the real expression gives \(2^4\cdot 3^2=16\cdot 9=\boxed{144}\).
Practice
Multiply \((7h^2)(2h^6)\) into a single term and enter its coefficient.
Show the solution
The exponents have nothing to do with the coefficient. Multiplying \(7\cdot 2\) gives the term \(14h^8\), so the coefficient is \(\boxed{14}\).
Practice
Multiply \((8p^3)(3p^4)\) into a single term. Enter the result, typed like \(5y^2\).
Show the solution
The tempting slip is adding the coefficients to get \(11p^7\). Coefficients are factors so they multiply, \(8\cdot 3=24\), while the exponents add, \(3+4=7\), giving \(\boxed{24p^7}\).
Practice
Simplify \(\frac{v^{16}}{v^{6}}\) to a single power \(v^k\) and enter \(k\).
Show the solution
The trap is answering 6, the number of factors that cancel. That count is not the exponent left behind. Six factors on the bottom cancel six of the sixteen on top, so the number of factors of \(v\) still standing is \(16-6=\boxed{10}\).
Practice
Simplify \(\frac{48t^{9}}{6t^{4}}\) to a single term. Enter it, typed like \(3y^2\).
Show the solution
Subtracting only the exponents and carrying the 48 along gives \(48t^5\), which leaves the number part untouched. The coefficients divide, \(48\div 6=8\), and the counts subtract, \(9-4=5\), giving \(\boxed{8t^5}\).
Practice
\((s^6)^7\) is seven copies of \(s^6\) multiplied together. Write it as \(s^k\) and enter \(k\).
Show the solution
Adding the exponents gives 13, but that is the product rule, and this is a power raised to a power. Each of the seven copies contributes six factors of \(s\), so the number of factors in the row is \(6\cdot 7=\boxed{42}\).
Practice
Expand \((5n^3)^3\) into a single term and enter its coefficient.
Show the solution
The trap is leaving the 5 alone and writing \(5n^9\). The outer exponent applies to every factor inside the parentheses, so \((5n^3)^3=5^3\cdot n^{3\cdot 3}=125n^9\), which makes the coefficient \(\boxed{125}\).
Practice
Simplify each of \((c^3)^8\) and \(c^3\cdot c^8\) to a single power of \(c\). Enter the exponent of the first minus the exponent of the second.
Show the solution
Reading both the same way gives 0, and that is the trap. Stacking eight copies of \(c^3\) multiplies the counts, so \((c^3)^8=c^{24}\), while setting two rows side by side adds them, so \(c^3\cdot c^8=c^{11}\). The difference is \(24-11=\boxed{13}\).
Practice
Expand \((j^5z^3)^3\) and enter the exponent on \(z\).
Show the solution
The 3 outside reaches both letters, not just the first one. Each of the three copies carries three \(z\) factors, so the result is \(j^{15}z^{9}\) and the exponent on \(z\) is \(\boxed{9}\).
Practice
Simplify \(\frac{(2m^5)^4}{8m^{11}}\) to a single term. Enter it, typed like \(7v^3\).
Show the solution
Forgetting \(2^4\) leaves \(2m^{20}\) on top and wrecks the coefficient. The parentheses clear to \(16m^{20}\), then \(16\div 8=2\) and \(20-11=9\), giving \(\boxed{2m^9}\).
