Algebra I · Lesson 1.5

Exponents

Solve this lesson, free →All lessons

Multiplication compresses repeated addition into one step. This lesson gives repeated multiplication the same treatment, a small raised number with a lot of reach, and every rule that number obeys comes from one habit, counting factors. Insisting the rules never break will then force the meaning of zero, negative, and fractional exponents.

Problem
Ten 2s multiplied together is written \(2^{10}\). Multiplying them out one at a time works, but it is slow. Instead split the ten factors into two equal groups of five, compute one group, then finish with a single multiplication. What is \(2^{10}\)?
Show a hint
  • One group of five 2s multiplies out to a number you know well.
Show the full solution
One at a time takes nine multiplications. The split needs five, four to find that five 2s make \(32\) and one more for the two groups, \(32\cdot 32=\boxed{1024}\).
Key idea
Definition. \(b^n\) means \(n\) factors of \(b\) multiplied together. The \(b\) is the base, the raised \(n\) is the exponent, and \(b^1\) is just \(b\), a single factor. Read \(2^{10}\) as 2 to the tenth. As in 1.2, an exponent binds only to the one symbol it touches. And the grouping move in the last problem, trading ten factors for two blocks of five, works far more generally, for a reason that gets a name shortly.
Problem
The product \(8\cdot 32\) is itself a power of 2. Each factor is itself a power of 2, so the whole product must be one too. Write \(8\cdot 32\) as \(2^x\) and enter \(x\).
Show a hint
  • Count the factors of 2 in each piece before multiplying anything.
Show the full solution
Multiplying out to \(256\) and factoring back down works, but counting is faster. \(8\) is three factors of 2 and \(32\) is five more, so the product is eight factors of 2 in one row, and \(x=\boxed{8}\).
Key idea
The product rule. \(a^m\cdot a^n=a^{m+n}\). The reason is a count. \(a^m\) is \(m\) factors of \(a\), \(a^n\) is \(n\) more, and setting them side by side makes one row of \(m+n\) factors. That is the whole proof, exponents add because factor counts add. One caution. The rule needs a shared base, since the count only works when every factor is the same number. \(2^3\cdot 5^2\) mixes 2s with 5s and combines into no single power.
Problem
Evaluate \(\frac{5^8}{5^5}\) without ever computing \(5^8\) or \(5^5\). Write the top and the bottom as strings of factors, cancel the shared ones, then multiply what is left.
Show a hint
  • How many factors of 5 are left after the cancelling?
Show the full solution
Brute force means \(390625\div 3125\), which works and wastes the structure. Five factors on the bottom cancel five of the eight on top, leaving \(5\cdot 5\cdot 5=\boxed{125}\).
Key idea
The quotient rule. \(\frac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}\). The same count, run in reverse. Each of the \(n\) factors on the bottom cancels a matching factor on top, and the subtraction \(m-n\) counts exactly what remains. That is why the last problem never needed the giant values, since \(\frac{5^8}{5^5}=5^{8-5}=5^3\) straight away. Like the product rule, this one requires a shared base, because only identical factors cancel.
Problem
The expression \((7^2)^3\) means three factors of \(7^2\). Written out, it is a single power of 7, so \((7^2)^3=7^x\) for some \(x\). Count the total number of 7s and enter \(x\).
Show a hint
  • Each copy of \(7^2\) contributes two 7s to the row.
Show the full solution
Adding to get \(7^{2+3}=7^5\) is the tempting wrong turn, since the 3 here counts copies rather than extra factors. Three copies of two 7s is \(2\cdot 3=6\) factors in all, so \(x=\boxed{6}\).
Key idea
Power of a power. \((a^m)^n=a^{mn}\), because \(n\) copies of \(m\) factors is \(mn\) factors, so this time the counts multiply. A cousin comes free. \((ab)^n=a^nb^n\), since \(n\) copies of \(ab\) reorder into \(n\) copies of \(a\) beside \(n\) copies of \(b\), and 1.3 says the reordering costs nothing. This rule is also why the opening split worked, since \(32^2=(2^5)^2=2^{5\cdot 2}=2^{10}\) exactly.
Problem
Walk down the powers of 3. \(3^4=81\), \(3^3=27\), \(3^2=9\), and each step down divides by 3. Keep the pattern going past \(3^1\) to \(3^0\). What value does the pattern force?
Show a hint
  • What is 3 divided by 3?
Show the full solution
The reflex answer for \(3^0\) is \(0\), but each step divides by 3, so \(3^2=9\) drops to \(3^1=3\), and one more step gives \(3\div 3\). The pattern forces \(3^0=\boxed{1}\).
Problem
The pattern does not stop at 1. Take two more steps down from \(3^0\), dividing by 3 at each step, to land on \(3^{-2}\). Enter its value as a fraction.
Show a hint
  • Each step still divides by 3, even after the values drop below 1.
Show the full solution
The minus sign tempts you toward \(-9\), which is the wrong turn. One step below \(1\) is \(\frac{1}{3}\), one more is \(\frac{1}{9}\), so \(3^{-2}=\boxed{\frac{1}{9}}\).
Key idea
Zero and negative exponents. For nonzero \(a\), \(a^0=1\) and \(a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}\). Nothing here is invented, these are the only values that keep the divide-by-the-base pattern intact, and the quotient rule lands in the same place, since \(\frac{3^2}{3^2}\) equals \(1\) by cancelling and \(3^{2-2}=3^0\) by the rule. The trap to avoid. A negative exponent makes a reciprocal, never a negative number, so \(3^{-2}\) is \(\frac{1}{9}\) and not \(-9\).
Problem
The product rule keeps working when an exponent is negative. Evaluate \(2^{-3}\cdot 2^7\) by adding the exponents before computing anything.
Show a hint
  • Add the exponents first. Only then evaluate the single power that remains.
Show the full solution
The long way computes \(2^{-3}=\frac{1}{8}\) and \(2^7=128\) separately, then divides \(128\) by \(8\). Adding first skips all of it, \(-3+7=4\), so the product is \(2^4=\boxed{16}\).
Problem
Suppose the power-of-a-power rule keeps working when an exponent is \(\frac{1}{2}\). Then \(\left(49^{1/2}\right)^2=49^{\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2}=49^1=49\), so \(49^{1/2}\) must be a number whose square is \(49\). What positive number is it?
Show a hint
  • Which positive number, times itself, gives \(49\)?
Show the full solution
\(-7\) also squares to \(49\), and the next block sorts out which of the two the notation names. The positive one is \(7\), since \(7\cdot 7=49\), so \(49^{1/2}=\boxed{7}\).
Key idea
Fractional exponents. \(a^{1/2}\) is defined as the nonnegative number whose square is \(a\), the familiar square root \(\sqrt{a}\). Nonnegative is part of the definition because both \(7\) and \(-7\) square to \(49\), and notation must name exactly one number, so the convention keeps the nonnegative one. Likewise \(a^{1/n}\) is the \(n\)th root of \(a\), and \(a^{m/n}=\left(a^{1/n}\right)^m\), root first, then power, which keeps the numbers small. Odd roots of negatives exist, since an odd count of negative factors stays negative, but an even root of a negative names no real number.
Problem
Evaluate \(9^{3/2}\). The exponent offers two orders, root first or cube first. Both are legal, but only one keeps every number small.
Show a hint
  • Write \(\frac{3}{2}\) as \(\frac{1}{2}\) applied first, then \(3\).
  • What is \(9^{1/2}\)?
Show the full solution
Cube first and you must handle \(\left(9^3\right)^{1/2}=729^{1/2}\), which is \(27\) since \(27\cdot 27=729\), but only after passing through \(729\). Root first stays small, \(9^{1/2}=3\) and \(3^3=27\), so \(9^{3/2}=\boxed{27}\).
Fun fact
Exponent notation is young. Descartes fixed the raised-number form in La Géométrie in 1637. Before that, writers spelled powers out in words, cubus for a third power, and computations ran on for lines.

One definition and one habit, counting factors, produced every rule here, and insisting those rules keep working gave \(a^0\), \(a^{-n}\), and \(a^{1/n}\) their meanings. Next, 1.6 returns to expressions and evaluates them at given values, and Chapter 3 brings variables into the laws you just built.

Practice these ideas

Practice
Warm up with the definition. \(5^4\) means four factors of \(5\) multiplied together, nothing more. Evaluate it as a single number.
Show the solution
Group the four \(5\)s into two pairs, each worth \(25\), so \(5^4 = 25 \cdot 25\). Multiplying one factor at a time works too but invites slips. The value is \(\boxed{625}\).
Practice
The product \(121 \cdot 11\) is itself a power of \(11\), since each of its two factors is one. Write the product as \(11^x\) and enter \(x\).
Show the solution
Since \(121 = 11^2\) and \(11 = 11^1\), the product has \(2 + 1 = 3\) factors of \(11\). The tempting slip is to multiply out to \(1331\) and enter that, but the problem asks for the exponent, not the product itself. So \(x = \boxed{3}\).
Practice
Evaluate \(\frac{12^9}{12^7}\). Cancel the shared factors before you multiply anything, and only then compute what is left.
Show the solution
Writing out \(12^9\), a ten-digit number, is the slow path. The seven shared factors of \(12\) cancel, leaving \(12^{9-7} = 12^2 = \boxed{144}\).
Practice
\((17^4)^3\) collapses to a single power of \(17\). Count the total factors of \(17\) it contains and enter the exponent \(x\) in \((17^4)^3 = 17^x\).
Show the solution
Each of the three copies of \(17^4\) contributes four factors of \(17\), so there are \(4 \cdot 3 = 12\) in all. Adding to get \(4 + 3 = 7\) confuses this with the product rule. So \(x = \boxed{12}\).
Practice
Evaluate \(14^0 \cdot 14^2\). One of the two factors is much simpler than it looks, so pin down its value before you multiply.
Show the solution
The reflex answer \(14^0 = 0\) is the trap. Any nonzero base to the zero power is \(1\). So the product is \(1 \cdot 14^2 = 1 \cdot 196 = \boxed{196}\).
Practice
Write \(5^{-2}\) as a fraction in lowest terms. Decide what the negative exponent actually does to the base before touching any arithmetic.
Show the solution
The wrong turns are \(-25\) and \(-\frac{1}{25}\). The minus in the exponent takes a reciprocal and never attaches a sign. \(5^{-2} = \frac{1}{5^2} = \boxed{\frac{1}{25}}\).
Practice
Evaluate \(15^{-4} \cdot 15^6\). Add the exponents before computing anything, then evaluate the small power that remains.
Show the solution
Working out \(15^6 = 11390625\) and dividing by \(15^4\) gets there the long way. The product rule gives \(15^{-4+6} = 15^2 = \boxed{225}\).
Practice
Evaluate \((-1)^{15} + (-1)^{16}\). Count the negative factors in each power, decide the sign each count forces, then add the two values.
Show the solution
Fifteen negative factors pair off with one left over, so \((-1)^{15} = -1\). Sixteen pair off exactly, so \((-1)^{16} = 1\). The trap is assuming a negative base always produces a negative result, which would give \(-1 + (-1) = -2\). The even exponent erases the sign, and the sum is \(-1 + 1 = \boxed{0}\).
Practice
Evaluate \(169^{1/2}\). The exponent names the one nonnegative number whose square is \(169\), so search for that number directly.
Show the solution
Both \(13\) and \(-13\) square to \(169\), but \(169^{1/2}\) names exactly one number, the nonnegative one. Answering \(-13\), or hedging with both, misses the convention. The value is \(\boxed{13}\).
Practice
Evaluate \((-27)^{1/3}\), the number whose cube is \(-27\). Decide first whether such a number can even exist, then find it.
Show the solution
An even root of a negative names no real number, but an odd root is fine, since an odd count of negative factors keeps the sign. \((-3)^3 = -27\), so \((-27)^{1/3} = \boxed{-3}\).
Practice
Evaluate \(256^{3/4}\). Take the fourth root first and cube the result, keeping every number in the computation small along the way.
Show the solution
Cubing first means finding \(256^3 = 16777216\) and then taking a fourth root, all through ugly numbers. Root first instead, \(256^{1/4} = 4\) since \(4^4 = 256\), and then \(4^3 = \boxed{64}\).
Practice
Find the number \(x\) that makes \(4^x = 8\) true. The powers of \(4\) you know jump right past \(8\), yet an \(x\) exists. Enter it as a fraction.
Show the solution
Hunting for a whole number fails, since \(4^1 = 4\) and \(4^2 = 16\) straddle \(8\). Write both sides in base \(2\), so \(4^x = 2^{2x}\) and \(8 = 2^3\), forcing \(2x = 3\). Then \(x = \boxed{\frac{3}{2}}\).
