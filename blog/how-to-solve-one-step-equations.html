How to Solve One-Step Equations

Aayan KhareJuly 24, 20266 min read

You are staring at something like \(x + 7 = 12\), and it feels like everyone else got a handbook you never received. There is a letter sitting where a number should be. Someone wants you to solve for \(x\), as if that phrase is supposed to mean anything. And a quiet voice in the back of your head has already reached its verdict. You are just not a math person.

Before you believe that voice, let me tell you the truth about one-step equations. They are one move. Not a talent, not a secret you were supposed to be born knowing. One move, and by the end of this you will see that you already make that kind of move all the time. Nobody ever showed you that it counts as math.

What that equals sign is actually saying

Most of the fear here comes from a misread. We spend years using the equals sign as a button, the thing on a calculator that means the answer goes next. In an equation it means something much calmer. It says the left side and the right side weigh exactly the same.

Picture an old balance scale, the kind with two pans that tip. The equation \(x + 7 = 12\) is telling you that the left pan, holding some mystery amount \(x\) plus \(7\) more, sits perfectly level with the right pan holding \(12\). Nothing is being asked yet. It is just a fact about balance. Your whole job is to find out what \(x\) weighs.

The one rule that runs everything

Here is the only rule you need, and it falls straight out of that scale. Whatever you do to one side, you do the exact same thing to the other side. Lift \(3\) off the left pan and the scale tips, unless you also lift \(3\) off the right. Keep both sides matched and the balance holds, which means the equation stays true the whole way down.

That is the entire game. Every one-step equation is just you doing one careful thing to both sides at once. And the goal of that one thing is always the same. You want to get the letter by itself. Solving for \(x\) means getting \(x\) alone on one side with a plain number on the other, so the equation finally reads \(x = \text{something}\). That something is your answer.

Undo the operation with its opposite

An equation traps \(x\) by doing one operation to it. It adds a number, or subtracts one, or multiplies, or divides. To set \(x\) free, you undo that operation with its opposite. Addition and subtraction are opposites. Multiplication and division are opposites. That is all inverse operation means, the move that cancels the other one out.

Here are all four. They are the same idea wearing four outfits.

Something was added to x $$x + 7 = 12$$

The \(7\) is stuck to \(x\) by addition, so subtract \(7\) from both sides. On the left the \(+7\) and \(-7\) cancel and leave \(x\) alone. On the right, \(12 - 7\) is \(5\).

$$x = 5$$
Something was subtracted from x $$x - 4 = 9$$

This time the equation took \(4\) away from \(x\), so you give it back. Add \(4\) to both sides. The \(-4\) and \(+4\) cancel on the left, and \(9 + 4\) is \(13\) on the right.

$$x = 13$$
x was multiplied $$3x = 15$$

Here \(3x\) means \(3\) times \(x\). Multiplication is undone by division, so divide both sides by \(3\). The \(3\)s cancel on the left, and \(15 \div 3\) is \(5\) on the right.

$$x = 5$$
x was divided $$\frac{x}{5} = 4$$

And \(\frac{x}{5}\) means \(x\) split into \(5\). Division is undone by multiplication, so multiply both sides by \(5\). The \(5\)s cancel on the left, and \(4 \times 5\) is \(20\) on the right.

$$x = 20$$

Four equations, one idea. Find the operation clinging to \(x\), and hit it with its opposite on both sides. Once you see that pattern, they stop being four separate things to memorize and become a single move you already understand.

The part that should kill the fear

Here is what almost nobody tells you, and it is the reason this should feel less scary than it looks. You never have to wonder whether you are right. You can check, every single time, and the equation will tell you the truth to your face.

Take your answer and put it back where \(x\) was. We solved \(x + 7 = 12\) and got \(x = 5\), so try it. Does \(5 + 7 = 12\)? Yes. The scale balances, so the answer holds. If you had slipped and gotten \(x = 4\), the check would say \(4 + 7 = 11\), which is not \(12\), and you would know instantly, on your own, with no answer key and no one to ask.

Being able to grade yourself is a strange kind of freedom. Most of the dread in math is the fear of being wrong and not knowing it. One-step equations hand you a way to erase that fear entirely. You stop guessing and hoping. You start checking and knowing.

You already do this

If the letters still make your stomach drop, try this. You solve equations like these in your head all the time. You just were never told that is what you were doing.

You had some cash, spent \(7\) dollars, and now you have \(5\) left. How much did you start with? You did not reach for a formula. You thought, well, \(5\) plus the \(7\) I spent, so \(12\). That is exactly \(x + 7 = 12\), solved cleanly by a person who was told their whole life they are bad at math. The symbols are not a new kind of thinking. They are shorthand for the thinking you were already doing.

One-step equations feel intimidating because of how they look, not because of what they ask. A letter and an equals sign got dressed up to look like a gate you are not allowed through. It was never a gate. It was one move, and you can make it. Then you make a slightly bigger one, and a slightly bigger one after that, and that is the whole of algebra, built out of moves you are fully capable of making today.
