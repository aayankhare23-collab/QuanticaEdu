Why Most Students Memorize Math Instead of Understanding It

Aayan KhareJuly 30, 20264 min read

You can solve the problem. If someone asks why the method works, you have nothing. That gap is not a sign you are bad at math. It is the thing school actually rewarded you for.

Most students memorize because memorizing pays. This post is about why it pays, the point where it stops, and two questions that tell you which one you are doing right now.

Memorizing is the rational choice

Look at what gets graded. The homework demonstrates a method. The test uses the same method with different numbers. A student who copies the method scores the same as a student who understands it, and finishes sooner.

Nobody is being lazy. Given that scoring system, memorizing is the correct play. It is also what most students are quietly trained to do, because a teacher with thirty students and a pacing guide has to keep moving.

Then it stops working

Arithmetic has maybe a dozen procedures. A dozen is holdable.

Algebra does not add more procedures so much as combine the ones you have. A single problem might need the distributive property, then a sign rule, then a step with fractions, in an order nobody announced. There is no method to recall, because the problem is a combination you have not seen before.

That is why the wall feels sudden and personal. Nothing changed about the student. The number of separate things to hold simply passed what holding can do.

Two questions that tell you which one you are doing

Can you say why it works in one sentence, without falling back on "that is just the rule"?

Can you still do it when the problem is not sitting under a heading that names the method?

If either answer is no, you have the procedure and not the idea. Worth knowing, and fixable.

A rule almost everyone memorizes

To divide by a fraction, flip it and multiply. Nearly every student can do this. Very few can say why it works.

Here is why. Division asks how many of these fit into that. So \(3 \div \tfrac{1}{2}\) is asking how many halves fit into 3. Two halves fit inside every 1, and there are three 1s, so six halves fit.

\(3 \div \tfrac{1}{2}\)→\(6\)

Multiplying by 2 gives 6 as well. Dividing by \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) and multiplying by 2 are the same question asked two ways. Flipping is not a trick someone invented. It is what the question already meant.

Understanding is fewer things to remember

This is the part that sounds backwards. Understanding feels like extra work stacked on top of the method you already have.

It is the opposite. That one reason covers dividing by any fraction, permanently. Without it you are carrying an unconnected rule that competes for space with every other unconnected rule you are carrying.

One idea replaces twenty rules. That is the entire trade, and it is why students who understand carry less, not more.

What to do about it

Ask why once per rule. Not every time you use it. Once, when it first appears. Two minutes on where a rule comes from buys you the rest of the year.

Try to break it. What happens if the number is negative? If it is zero? A rule you have pushed on is a rule you understand.

Do problems out of order. Mixed practice strips away the heading that tells you which method to reach for, which is the only honest test of whether you know it.

None of this requires more study time. It requires spending a little of it on the reason instead of all of it on the steps.
